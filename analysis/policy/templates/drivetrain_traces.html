Balance: sim vs bike

A balance control policy, standing still and holding its heading: in simulation on the left, on the real bike on the right. If the simulation is accurate, the two columns look alike in terms of sway, small corrections, and shake. Differences indicate shortfalls in the model. Pick a run at the top of each column; lines are measured, dashed lines are commanded. More at the bottom of the page.

More info

The bike

  • It balances by steering and by its rear wheel. The rear wheel is an omni wheel: rollers around its rim let the back of the bike move sideways. Two drive motors turn it; when they turn in step the bike rolls forward, and the difference between them spins the rollers. A policy (a small neural network trained in simulation) reads the bike's sensors and sets the steering and both motors 50 times a second; the servos are read and logged at 100 Hz.
  • Gear notches and play. The drivetrain is not smooth. The gearbox has a notchy feel, a small catch every 7.5° of its output ring (the grey lines in the roller panel), and there is slack in the rollers, the axles and the steering head. A policy trained on a model that gets these slightly wrong can work in simulation and still shake on the real bike. This page is how those effects were found and put back into the model.

The policies

  • MLP. An early policy, a plain feed-forward network, trained on plant V1. Shakes at about 11 Hz on the real bike.
  • minGRU. The other three are small recurrent networks (minGRU), named by the plant, the simulated bike, each was trained on.
  • Plant V1. The bike model before its current geometry, with the first drivetrain model: a friction bump at each gear notch, and a first fit of the slack in the rollers. Its two bike runs, sample 1 and sample 2, are two days apart.
  • Plant V2. The current geometry, with the same first drivetrain model. Balances in simulation; shakes on the bike like V1.
  • Plant V3, the current one. V2 plus the roller slack refitted, the gear notches as the sawtooth measured on the bench, and flex at the axles and the steering head, fitted to the bike's own floor runs. On the bike the shake is gone.

What each policy is simulated on

  • Plant, then policy. Each simulation names the plant it runs on, then the policy flying it. Every policy is flown on plant V2 and on plant V3, whichever it was trained on. Plant V1 is never flown: every simulation here is on the current geometry, so for the MLP and the plant V1 policy the nearest is V2, which has the same drivetrain they trained with.

The panels

  • Roll. How far the bike leans. One scale for every run, so the columns compare directly.
  • Yaw. The bike's heading minus the heading it was told to hold.
  • Steer. The steering angle and the angle the policy asked for (dashed).
  • Drive difference. The speed of one drive motor less the other's: what spins the rollers. Measured and commanded.
  • Roller angle. Each roller's angle, wrapped to one cycle, so a roller resting on a gear notch shows as a flat line on a grey line. The thick line is where the rollers would be with no slack at all; the orange segment is whichever roller is on the floor, which only the simulation knows. The phase slider rotates the wrap by whole notches.
  • The small spectrum plots. How much shaking there is at each frequency from 0 to 25 Hz, from the rate of each signal. Bright is this column's window, faded the other column's; window is this column's alone, all its whole run; the dot marks the strongest peak above 4 Hz (below that is ordinary balancing sway). All the plots of one signal share a scale, so their heights compare across columns.

Reading the two columns

  • Recorded the same way. The simulation's signals are made as the bike records them: the same sensors, the same resolution, 100 samples a second. A difference between the columns is a difference between the bikes, not in how they were measured.
  • The commanded drive is reconstructed. The motors do not report where they are being driven to, so in both columns it is worked back from the power they draw. That makes it noisy above about 20 Hz: compare it column to column, not against the measured line.
  • Not the same moment. The simulation and the bike are separate runs, and their seconds do not line up. Compare the character of the motion, not individual wiggles.
  • Controls. Window sets how many seconds are shown, and the slider at the top of each column moves them through its run; sync sim+real time sliders moves both columns together. Drag across a plot to zoom; double-click to go back.
