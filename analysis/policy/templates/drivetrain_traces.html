Balance: sim vs bike

The same trained balance controller, standing still and holding its heading: in simulation on the left, on the real bike on the right. If the simulation is a faithful copy of the bike, the two columns look alike: the same slow sway, the same small corrections, the same amount of shake. Where they differ, the model is missing something. Pick a run at the top of each column; lines are measured, dashed lines are commanded. More at the bottom of the page.

More info

The bike

  • It balances by steering and by its rear wheel. The rear wheel is an omni wheel: rollers around its rim let the back of the bike move sideways. Two drive motors turn it; when they turn in step the bike rolls forward, and the difference between them spins the rollers. A policy (a small neural network trained in simulation) reads the bike's sensors and sets the steering and both motors 50 times a second; the servos are read and logged at 100 Hz.
  • Gear notches and play. The drivetrain is not smooth. The gearbox has a notchy feel, a small catch every 7.5° of its output ring (the grey lines in the roller panel), and there is slack in the rollers, the axles and the steering head. A policy trained on an idealised drivetrain can work in simulation and still shake on the real bike. This page is how those effects were found and put back into the model.

The policies

  • Policy A. An early policy, trained on an earlier version of the bike model. Shakes at about 11 Hz on the real bike.
  • Policy B. Trained on the bike model before its current geometry, with a smooth drivetrain.
  • Policy C. The current geometry, still with a smooth drivetrain. Balances in simulation; shakes on the bike like B.
  • Policy D, the current one. Trained with the measured gear notches and the slack in the simulation. On the bike the shake is gone.

The simulated drivetrains

  • As trained. Exactly the drivetrain model the policy learned on.
  • Measured gear notches + roller slop. The notch shape measured on the bench, and slack in each roller, added to it.
  • Wheel play. A little looseness at the axles and the steering head, fitted to the bike's own floor runs.

The panels

  • Roll. How far the bike leans. One scale for every run, so the columns compare directly.
  • Yaw. The bike's heading less the heading it was told to hold.
  • Steer. The steering angle and the angle the policy asked for (dashed).
  • Drive difference. The speed of one drive motor less the other's: what spins the rollers. Measured and commanded.
  • Roller angle. Each roller's angle, wrapped to one turn, so a roller resting on a gear notch shows as a flat line on a grey line. The thick line is where the rollers would be with no slack at all; the orange segment is whichever roller is on the floor, which only the simulation knows. The phase slider rotates the wrap by whole notches.
  • The small spectrum plots. How much shaking there is at each frequency from 0 to 25 Hz, from the rate of each signal. Bright is this column's window, dim the other column's, the last one the whole run; the dot marks the strongest peak above 4 Hz (below that is ordinary balancing sway). All the plots of one signal share a scale, so their heights compare across columns.

Reading the two columns

  • Recorded the same way. The simulation's signals are made as the bike records them: the same sensors, the same resolution, 100 samples a second. A difference between the columns is a difference between the bikes, not in how they were measured.
  • The commanded drive is reconstructed. The motors do not report where they are being driven to, so in both columns it is worked back from the power they draw. That makes it noisy above about 20 Hz: compare it column to column, not against the measured line.
  • Not the same moment. The simulation and the bike are separate runs, and their seconds do not line up. Compare the character of the motion, not individual wiggles.
  • Controls. Window sets how many seconds are shown, start slides through the run. Drag across a plot to zoom; double-click to go back.
